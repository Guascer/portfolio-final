The Next Step Planter

A self-watering planter designed as a personality self-portrait.

Introduction to Product & UX Design · Griffith University · 2026 · 220 × 110 × 100 mm · Foam, PLA, timber
The brief

A self-portrait, coded not guessed

The brief was to design a product as a self-portrait, using the OCEAN personality model. I coded a written account of a turning point in my own life into 22 personality codes. Conscientiousness came out far ahead — 20 mentions, against 13 each for Openness and Neuroticism and 9 each for Extraversion and Agreeableness — and that is the measured reason the body is rectilinear and stepped rather than a matter of taste.

User testing

What the numbers actually showed

Three questions in a 5-point Likert survey asked whether the form, colour and material semantics matched the intended personality. 34 people answered. Colour landed clearly (91.2% agreed), form read as calm but not distinctly personal (61.8%), and material and finish was the weakest and most divided (52.9%).

Contextual research

Studied for mechanism, rejected on form

I studied existing self-watering planters for the mechanism, and deliberately rejected them on form. Every reference is round and ribbed; the personality data pointed the other way.

Sketches & form exploration

Sketch and form exploration

The first sketch already had the steps, the soil opening and a small face on the indicator. I then blocked the form out quickly in Blender before building it properly.

Technical drawings

Drawn at full size, on purpose

Three 1:1 sheets printed on A3 and laid straight under the foam block, so the outline could be transferred at full size without measuring errors.

Assembly sequence

The foam seat came out smaller and slightly out of round, so I measured the real opening and revised the inner pot to match: top Ø 83.25 mm, height 73.98 mm. The physical part became the source of truth and the CAD followed it.

Digital prototype

Three parts, modelled separately

Modelled parametrically in Autodesk Fusion as separate parts: body, inner pot and indicator.

How it works

No electronics, no batteries

01

Fill the reservoir

Water goes in around the inner pot, not onto the soil directly.

02

Seat the inner pot

Four timber pins hold it 3 mm clear of the reservoir floor.

03

Water reaches the roots

Perforations in the inner pot base let it wick up into the soil.

04

Watch the indicator

A foam float rises when full and sinks when empty.

No electronics, no batteries. Archimedes keeps the indicator floating, sub-irrigation lets the soil draw only the water it needs. The state is visible without being read: head up means full, head down means empty.

Physical prototype

Six pieces of foam, one hand-mixed grey

Built from six laminated pieces of high density foam, shaped by hot wire and spindle sander, glued with PVA and held with tape because foam crushes under clamps. The workshop only had white and black paint, so the grey was mixed by hand.

Building found what modelling hid. In Fusion the base holes looked finished, because a model has no contact and no gravity. Only with the printed pot in my hand was it obvious they opened onto a solid floor. Four timber pins fixed it.

Final design & reflection

This is an appearance and mechanism prototype. Foam proves the geometry at full size (reservoir volume, clearance under the pot, indicator travel) but it is porous and cannot hold water. Rebuilding the same geometry in a printed or moulded polymer body is the one step between this object and a working planter.

What I take with me: treat the digital model as a proposal that the physical build tests, not as the finished answer.
